Research Question

The Question that Began with a Plate

When I was a child, porcelain did not first appear to me as an academic subject. It appeared as an enchanting mystery: how could a fragile object carry so much of a civilization inside it? A bowl could be plain and still feel ceremonial; a blue line could suggest trade routes, minerals, and stories of the craftsmen.

I began with one question: how has China developed such an early and enduring ceramic tradition? But the question soon widened beyond China itself. The more I looked, the less porcelain seemed like a national cultural possession. Cobalt travelled before it became known as blue-and-white. Islamic metalwork reappeared as Chinese porcelain form. European courts collected, mounted, imitated, and eventually remade the materials they once could only input. What began as childhood wonder became a research problem: how do objects preserve cultural identity while absorbing the world around them?

Core Evidence

The Research Core: Ming-Qing Ceramic Shifts

I collected 148 scored core samples and evaluated them based on 18 datasets across three regions, three roles, and two periods, for an ultimate goal of tracing whether cross-regional ceramic fusion continued to intensify from Song-Yuan precedents, or changed its direction. The quantitative result demonstrates that the Ming-era ceramic exchange still bears strong influences from the Middle East and West Asia, while Qing-era exchange shifts toward a direct China-Europe axis.

For China, the Ming period shows deep absorption of Islamic materials and forms. Persian cobalt (Sumali blue), Islamic metalwork prototypes such as moon flasks, and arabesque or geometric motifs entered Jingdezhen’s decorating techniques. By the Qing, earlier Islamic elements had been internalized, which incentivized ceramic output toward Europe in products containing Western dining forms, famille rose palettes, rococo compositions through the modern Guangdong province.

In the Qing dynasty, ceramic exchange underwent a shift: the primary axis of cross-cultural circulation moved away from the historic Middle Eastern/West Asian intermediary network toward direct China–Europe trade. Having fully internalized earlier Islamic stylistic influences, Chinese porcelain production increasingly catered to European tastes, while the Middle East and West Asia lost their historic role as the critical hinge of global ceramic exchange.

The Project’s Methodological Innovation

Most ceramic histories follow one route. This project compares China, Middle East / West Asia, and Europe side by side, then separates output objects, input objects, and local or local-absorption samples.

Local / Local Absorption

Native workshops internalize outside elements until they become local ceramic language.

Byzantine/European glazed bowl baseline.

Input / Input

Receivers collect, alter, mount, imitate, or reinterpret foreign ceramics and technologies.

Yuan-style blue-and-white dragon/phoenix dish; cobalt-color pivot linking China and Islamic material networks.

Output / Output

Makers adapt goods for outside markets and new systems of use.

West Asian blue-and-white/fritware context from thesis sample table; use to compare input Chinese chromatic language.

The Thousand-Year Arc Beyond Ming-Qing

Song ceramics establish a baseline of local refinement; Yuan exchange accelerates the material conditions for later fusion; Republic, modern, and contemporary ceramics reorganize heritage through reform, institutions, markets, and reflective global practice.

Expanded L8 Findings

Key Findings

Authentic Chapter 8 evidence is reorganized into a clean admissions-facing argument: all conclusions and explanation stay in the left column, while all visual evidence appears separately in the right column.

01

Ming-to-Qing change is network restructuring, not simple growth.

Across the Ming-to-Qing transition, ceramic cultural exchange did not simply grow along one continuous line. The pattern changed structurally: the Ming network centered on Middle East / West Asia links, while the Qing network shifted toward a direct China-Europe axis.

Network restructuring
02

The Middle East / West Asia hub weakened as direct China-Europe routes strengthened.

The Qing pattern shows two linked changes: China-Europe exchange strengthened, while the Middle East / West Asia intermediary position weakened. These are two sides of the same network shift, not separate coincidences.

Direct axis replaces relay
03

Three formerly “double-saturated” relationships disappeared in Qing.

In the Ming dataset, three regional relationships were “double-saturated.” By the Qing, all three had disappeared, replaced by a more mixed structure of absorption, input-driven exchange, and near-equal relations.

Relationship pattern shift
04

The Ming Gap explains why Ming evidence is uneven.

The thesis treats the Ming Gap as a central explanatory variable. The Ming period combines a long maritime-ban rupture (1368-1567) with a later export surge after the 1567 reopening, so its scores should not be read as a smooth export peak.

Historical caution
05

The largest increases and decreases support the network-restructuring interpretation.

The largest increases occur in China receiving Europe (+4.6), the Middle East / West Asia receiving Europe (+3.5), and China exporting to Europe (+3.1). The largest decreases occur where Middle East / West Asia mediation weakens: China receiving Middle East / West Asia (-3.0) and Europe receiving Middle East / West Asia (-2.6).

Delta evidence
06

Local absorption can stay stable even when trade channels change.

Even while export and import channels changed sharply, the strongest forms of local absorption remained stable. Once local fusion is embedded in materials, vessel forms, and decorative grammar, it can persist across periods.

Stable local absorption
07

The five-dimensional framework diagnoses different regional behaviors.

The five-dimensional method is not just a scoring tool. It shows that Europe, the Middle East / West Asia, and China absorbed and transformed ceramic influence through different dominant dimensions.

5D research design
08

Each region has a distinct behavior label.

Europe is input-driven: it physically and imaginatively reworked imported porcelain. The Middle East / West Asia is absorption-based: color and function became embedded in an aesthetic system. China shows absorption and movement toward parity as imported color materials and Islamic vessel forms were internalized into a Chinese system.

Regional behavior labels

Author Reflection

“What began as a childhood question became a way of reading the world through objects. I learned that a ceramic bowl is never only a bowl: it can be a mineral route, an imperial preference, a market calculation, a religious echo, or a misunderstanding made beautiful. The project taught me to treat beauty not as decoration, but as evidence.”
